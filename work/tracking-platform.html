01

Overview

The challenge

The same LEGO set can cost very different amounts depending on the shop and the week. LEGO BLOCK follows the prices of 5,893+ sets across multiple retailers, so enthusiasts don’t have to. My job was to turn that pile of scattered price data into answers a collector can act on in seconds.

"Users were drowning in tabs and spreadsheets just to find the best price for one set."

The existing solution was a manual process — open multiple retailer sites, compare prices, track changes over time. The goal was to design a platform that does all of that automatically and surfaces the right information at the right moment.

What “tracking a set” actually means

Tracking isn’t a single screen — it’s how every set is presented across the platform. Three layers turn raw prices into something a collector can trust.

  1. 1

    Collect

    Each set shows its current prices from multiple retailers, lined up next to its recommended retail price (RRP).

  2. 2

    Remember

    Each set has a price history chart — so “cheap” can be judged against the set’s own past, not a guess.

  3. 3

    Surface

    Changes come back as signals: green or red change badges, the Recent Changes feed, Best Deals rankings and the price chart on each set.

Reading a set at a glance

Every card and table in the platform uses the same four numbers, so once you learn them on one screen, you can read them everywhere.

Now
The current price shown for the set.
Change
How far the price moved, in $ and % — green when it’s cheaper, red when it went up.
RRP
The official retail price, the baseline every deal is measured against.
Price per brick
Price divided by piece count, to compare value across sets of different sizes.

Why a collector cares

The casual collector

Knows in one glance whether a “sale” is actually a deal — without opening ten retailer tabs.

The serious investor

Sees the full price history of a set before buying or selling, instead of relying on memory or spreadsheets.

The gifter

Finds where a set is cheapest and what the total will be, so the gift is sorted in minutes.

Who it's for

Three collectors. Three different questions.

Research surfaced three core user types with very different mental models and jobs-to-be-done. Rather than design for one generic “LEGO fan”, every page answers the question one of them walks in with.

The Casual Collector

“Is this a good deal right now?”

Browses for fun and buys when the price feels right. Has no patience for spreadsheets or ten open retailer tabs.

LEGO Block answers with

  • Best Deals ranked by discount type
  • Price-change badges on every set
  • One tap to save a set for later

“Where is this set’s price heading?”

Treats LEGO as an asset. Tracks retired sets over months and buys or sells on price history, not on impulse.

LEGO Block answers with

  • Price history chart on every Set Overview
  • Store-by-store comparison table

03

Problem → Solution

Six problems the redesign had to solve.

Each screen starts from one collector’s question. Here is what got in the way of answering it, and the design decision that removed it.

01
The Casual Collector asks“Is this a good deal right now?”
Problem

A price with no reference point

A price on its own means nothing. Without a reference point, collectors open retailer after retailer just to check whether a discount is real.

Solution

Every price carries its context

The current price sits right next to how far it moved — in $ and % — green when it drops, red when it rises. No maths, no extra tabs.

02
The Serious Investor asks“Where is this set’s price heading?”
Problem

Price changes slip past unnoticed

Prices move between visits. Spotting what changed meant re-checking the same sets one by one.

Solution

One feed for every movement

Recent Changes collects the latest price movements across sets in a single scannable grid, each with its change badge.

03
The Serious Investor asks“Where is this set’s price heading?”
Problem

Sets of different sizes are hard to compare

A small set and a 3,000-piece set can’t be judged on price alone — the cheaper one isn’t always the better buy.

Solution

Value, not just price

The New Sets table puts price per brick and RRP side by side in sortable columns, so value is comparable across any set size.

04
The Casual Collector asks“Is this a good deal right now?”
Problem

Every deal looks the same

A long list of discounted sets blurs together; the genuinely strong offers get lost in the noise.

Solution

Deals ranked at a glance

Best Deals uses colour-coded badges by discount type, so the strongest offers stand out before you read a single number.

05
The Gifter asks“Which set should I buy — and where is it cheapest?”
Problem

Browsing and personal tracking were tangled

Discovery and “my sets” lived in the same places, so it was unclear where to go to explore versus where to check what you follow.

Solution

Two clear modes in the navigation

Browsing lives in Recent Changes, New Sets and Best Deals. Tracking is personal — Likes and Cart are yours alone. The split makes navigation obvious without onboarding.

Browse
  • Recent Changes
  • New Sets
  • Best Deals

Passive discovery — what's moving in the market.

Track
  • Likes
  • Cart

Personal — the sets you follow and plan to buy.

06
All three collectorsEvery question above, on one screen
Problem

Dense data felt overwhelming

Thousands of sets, prices and changes on one screen quickly turns into a wall of numbers.

Solution

A strict visual hierarchy

White cards on a dark background, yellow as the only action colour, red and green reserved for price signals. You always know what matters most.

04

Brand

Built from the brick up

Before touching a single screen, I established a visual language rooted in LEGO's own DNA. Yellow is the hero — the same vibrant hue that's been on every LEGO box since 1958. The supporting palette pulls directly from iconic LEGO brick colours.

Brand colours mapped to real LEGO brick colours — yellow, blue, grey, green, red.

Typeface — Inter

Inter carries the functional load. Clean, legible at every size, and neutral enough to step back and let the yellow accents and product photography do the talking. Used from 10px data labels all the way to 80px hero headings.

06

Process

Research & discovery

I mapped three core user types — the casual collector who wants a quick deal, the serious investor tracking price history, and the gifter trying to find something specific fast. Every page in the architecture is tied back to one of their questions.

Architecture & flows

The key decision was separating browsing from tracking. Browsing lives in Recent Changes, New Sets, and Best Deals — passive discovery. Tracking is personal: the wishlist and likes pages are yours alone. This split made navigation intuitive without any onboarding.

UI design

White cards on a dark editorial background. Yellow as the single action colour — every button, badge, and highlight uses it. Price changes get red or green. Everything else is neutral. The visual hierarchy is almost mathematical: you always know what matters most.

07

Key Screens

Walking through the platform

Each screen solves one problem clearly. Price changes are the heartbeat of the platform — so Recent Changes is always one click away from anywhere. The Set Overview page goes deep: price history chart, store comparison, piece count, availability. Everything a collector needs to make the call.

"Every screen answers exactly one question. Nothing more, nothing less."

The brand in the wild — LEGO's own red, yellow and white.

The palette borrows straight from LEGO's signage.
